Food-Grade Filter Paper: a B2B Buying Checklist

Five things to confirm with any supplier before your first food-contact order.

Filter paper that touches food — tea bags, coffee pods, milk and juice filtration, frying oil — carries a level of scrutiny that industrial media never sees. One compliance gap can hold a shipment at customs or worse, trigger a recall. The good news: the checks that protect you are few and straightforward. Run this list with any prospective supplier, including us.

1. Food-contact compliance, documented

"Food grade" means nothing on its own; the declaration behind it does. Ask which framework the mill certifies against — for exports the usual references are the EU Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 framework plus relevant national measures, and FDA 21 CFR for the US market — and request the actual declaration of compliance, not a summary. A serious mill produces these documents with the shipment, in your product's name, for the exact paper grade you are buying.

  • Written declaration of compliance naming the product and grade
  • Test reports from an accredited lab where applicable (migration, heavy metals)
  • Clarity on additives: wet-strength resins and releases must themselves be food-contact compliant

2. Wet strength that matches your process

Filter paper is engineered to fail in a controlled way — tea bags must burst to release flavor, while milk filter bags must hold a full load without tearing. These opposite behaviors come from fiber furnish and wet-strength treatment. Tell the supplier how the paper fails in your process: a tea producer wants consistent burst; a broth producer wants zero burst. If your current paper behaves exactly right, supply a sample — matching behavior is far easier than describing it.

3. Flow rate and breathability for your line speed

On automated packing and filling lines, paper that filters beautifully but flows slowly throttles the whole line. Flow-related properties to compare: air permeability (for tea bag and dry applications), liquid flow time (for drip and liquid filtration), and porosity consistency across the roll. Ask for these numbers on the data sheet — if a supplier cannot produce them, their "food grade" paper is probably a general-purpose paper with a label.

4. Consistency, batch after batch

Food production punishes variation. A grade that shifts between shipments changes your fill times, your seal quality and sometimes your product itself. Before committing to volume:

  • Run a pilot lot on your line — most mills support this for qualified buyers
  • Agree the specification in writing: weight (g/m²), thickness, burst, permeability ranges
  • Confirm the mill keeps retention samples so any later dispute can be tested against the actual batch

5. Formats and packaging that fit your machine

The same paper ships as parent rolls, slit rolls, sheets, discs or die-cut blanks. Specify roll width tolerance, core size and winding direction for your machine, and food-safe wrapping (moisture-barrier liner, palletized and shrink-wrapped) for transit — especially for sea freight to humid markets. We cut and die-cut to drawing, and confirm the cutting layout with you before production to minimize offcut waste.

A note on tea bag and coffee applications specifically

These are the two categories where material choice is most specialized: heat-sealable and non-heat-sealable tea bag papers behave differently on packing machines; PLA (corn-based biopolymer) and PET mesh alternatives serve compostable and pyramid formats; drip coffee papers balance flow and fines retention differently for flat-bottom vs cone formats. None of this is exotic — but all of it is easier to get right when the buyer describes the machine and the end format, not just "tea bag paper".

Bottom line

Compliance documents, honest data sheets, a pilot run and a written specification — four steps that take a week and prevent the four most expensive mistakes in food-contact filtration.
